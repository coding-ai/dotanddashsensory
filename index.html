Where this started

What does high contrast actually mean?

It turns out to be a phrase that covers a lot of ground.

A soft grey pattern and a true black-and-white one can both be described the same way, and to a six-week-old they are quite different things. Working out where the line sits is what sent us down this road in the first place. Since we had to measure it anyway, we may as well publish what we found — the values live in the code, so every video is made the same way.

See every value →
01

Contrast ratio

Measured at 18.96:1, against 21:1 for absolute black on white. We stop deliberately short of pure values: absolute black gives the encoder no headroom and hard edges ring after compression.

02

Pattern spacing

Our repeating patterns run 0.08–0.35 cycles per degree, which sits inside the band where infant contrast sensitivity peaks. It shifts with your screen and where your baby is sitting, so we have worked it out for a few real setups.

03

Motion speed

Capped at 8–11°/s for 0–3 months, rising to about 13°/s by 6–12 months. For the youngest babies most of the video simply rests, and when something does move it moves slowly enough to follow.

04

Flicker

The highest reading across the whole catalogue is 0.56 qualifying transitions per second, against the general-population threshold of three in WCAG 2.3.1. No infant-specific standard exists, so we use the closest published one and keep a wide margin.

05

Scene length

Every video is built on a musical bar grid at 48–116 BPM by age, so scene changes land on a downbeat. A newborn scene runs 20–90 seconds. Babies settle into a pattern and gradually lose interest, and that whole arc is the part worth having, so scenes are given room to breathe.

Good to know

Questions

Short answers. The long version lives in the specification.

Why do newborns respond to black and white?

A newborn's contrast sensitivity is very low compared with an adult's, so soft or pastel patterns register as roughly uniform grey. Black against white is the largest luminance difference available, which is why it holds attention when gentler colours do not.

How far can a newborn actually see?

Clearest vision at birth is at roughly 20 to 30 centimetres — about the distance from a feeding baby's eyes to the face of whoever is holding them. Detail beyond that falls away quickly in the first weeks.

When can babies see colour?

Colour vision emerges gradually over the first months rather than switching on at a fixed date. Red is generally distinguished before green and blue, and by around five to six months colour discrimination is much closer to an adult's.

How long should a high-contrast session be?

Let the baby end it rather than the clock. Turning away, glazing over, fussing or arching are all signals they have had enough, and at this age a few minutes of engaged looking is a complete session.

How far away should the screen be?

There is no single right answer, so we publish the maths instead of a rule. A phone at 40 cm, a tablet at 50 cm and a TV at 2.5 m all land within a fairly narrow band — roughly 74 to 97 pixels per degree of your baby's visual field — so the patterns work across all three. The full table is on the methodology page.

Are high-contrast cards better than videos?

For most families under six months, cards are the lovely option. No screen, you can hold them at exactly the right distance, and your baby decides how long to look. Video earns its place on the days when your hands are full.

Is screen time safe for a newborn?

Paediatric guidance generally discourages screen media below around 18 to 24 months, apart from video calls. We think that is sound, and we would rather say so. What we can offer is a clear account of what is on the screen, for the moments when a family does reach for one.

What changes at three months?

Detail resolution improves, tracking becomes smoother, and colour starts to come in. That is where our tier 2 videos begin: a single red is introduced, and nothing else. Yellow and blue are held back until the 6–12 month tier, and motion speeds rise from roughly 8°/s to 13°/s across the three bands.
